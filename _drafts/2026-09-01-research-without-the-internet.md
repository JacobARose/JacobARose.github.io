I recently started as Technology and Digital Pedagogy Coordinator at the NYU Institute of Human Development and Social Change, supporting the [NYU Prison Education Program](https://prisoneducation.nyu.edu/). My job is to help close the digital literacy divide in college-in-prison education. I want to write down how I'm thinking about that phrase at the start, partly so I can look back later and see what I got wrong.

## What kind of divide it is

When people hear "digital divide," they usually picture a slow connection, or an old laptop, or a household choosing between broadband and something else. Those are real problems. But they all describe the same axis, with everyone somewhere along it, more or less connected to the same world.

College-in-prison education sits somewhere off that axis entirely. Students work in environments where the open internet isn't available at all, by design and by policy. What exists instead is a substitute stack: offline intranet systems, locally hosted course platforms, computer labs with fixed hardware, library technologies, and a set of research tools that have been reviewed and approved for use. None of that is the internet with the speed turned down. It's a different information environment with its own shape. So the question I keep circling is what college-level research looks like when every workflow you'd normally teach assumes a search box connected to everything.

## Two gaps, not one

The more I look at it, the more it seems like two separate problems that get collapsed into one phrase.

The first is the **access gap**, which is the concrete one. If a student is writing a paper and the source they need isn't on the intranet, isn't in the offline collection, and isn't in the approved tool set, then for their purposes it doesn't exist. A lot of my early work is unglamorous inventory. What's installed, what's working, what can be reached from a lab terminal, and where the holes are between what a syllabus assumes and what a student can physically retrieve.

The second is the **transfer gap**, and it's the one I find harder. The research skills that are practical inside are genuinely rigorous ones: knowing a collection well, working within a curated set of sources, planning ahead because you can't look something up on impulse. Some are arguably better intellectual habits than the ones people pick up with infinite tabs open. But they aren't the skills the same student will need the week after release, when the environment flips to an overwhelming, unfiltered, algorithmically sorted one. Teach only for the inside environment and you set people up for a cliff. Teach only for the outside one and it's abstract to the point of useless while they're still inside.

## What I'm doing about it right now

Very little of this is theoretical. The early work:

- **Assessment.** Documenting the technological infrastructure that supports incarcerated students, from intranet systems and lab hardware to learning platforms, library technologies, and approved research tools, then mapping where the gaps limit access to academic material. This is much of what I'm doing at the moment.
- Co-designing surveys, focus groups, and infrastructural assessments with students and faculty. I'm a month in. The people who have been teaching and studying in this context already know what breaks; my job is to collect that systematically rather than arrive with a solution.
- **Writing it down**, in the form of a programmatic knowledge base of approved technologies, technical skills, and offline research resources, so a faculty member designing a course or a student starting a project doesn't have to rediscover the constraints from scratch every semester.
- **Coordination** with the NYU Division of Libraries, the New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision, and peer college-in-prison providers. Several institutions are working on overlapping pieces of this in parallel.
- Developing resources for formerly incarcerated students continuing their education after release, which is where the transfer gap stops being abstract.

## The part I have to unlearn

My background is machine learning, data engineering, and physics. In that world you assume a package index, a docs site, a GPU you can reach, a Stack Overflow answer waiting for whatever error message you just hit. Nearly every tool I'm fluent in was built on the premise that the network is there.

The instinct that does transfer, I think, is the experimental one: characterize the channel before you try to send anything through it. In my graduate work that meant measuring a noisy physical medium carefully enough to know what it could carry. Here the medium is institutional rather than physical, but the discipline is the same, and the alternative is designing for an environment that doesn't exist.

More once the assessment work turns up things I didn't see coming.
