I recently started a new role as the Technology and Digital Pedagogy Coordinator at the NYU Institute of Human Development and Social Change, supporting the [NYU Prison Education Program](https://prisoneducation.nyu.edu/). My job is to help close the digital literacy divide between incarcerated students and their peers on campus. My goal here is to record my initial understanding of these technological challenges facing students pursuing an education while constrained by a prison environment.

## What kind of divide it is

The "digital divide"^[citation needed] when it comes to prisons is a useful term for understanding many of the ways the prison system fails to equip prisoners with the digital knowledge and skills they need to survive upon release in to a world heavily dependent upon technology.

In most U.S. prisons, incarcerated people are prohibited or heavily restricted from accessing the open internet. Imagine completing an Associates or Bachelors degree with little to no access to google or relevant literature to perform your coursework. Upon release, they are often immediately expected to know how to use the internet in order to apply for jobs or to access housing, banking and benefits. These are avoidable gaps in a system designed for rehabilitative justice, and failing to address them results in higher rates of recidivism^[citation needed].

The question I'm interested in answeringis what college-level research can look like when most curricula assume students have instant, unrestricted access to the internet.

## Two gaps, not one

The more I look at it, the more it seems like there are at least two separate problems that fall under the category of a digital divide.

The first is the **access gap**, which is the result of restrictions on information made available to students while in prison. If a student is writing a paper and the source they need is neither on the intranet nor in an offline collection, then for their purposes it doesn't exist. There are some efforts to make catalogs of articles available for students within prison, but these often require them to submit a request and wait for a printed copy to be delivered to them before ever reading past the abstract. Their peers on the outside can go to Anna's Archive and download a dozen of the most relevant papers in response to even the smallest intellectual curiosity. These discrepancies in information access inherently increase the demands incarcerated students must meet in order to complete their degree.

The second is the **transfer gap**, which is in some ways a more complicated problem. The skills necessary for thriving in the outside world are not automatically imparted upon people at the end of their sentence. The research skills developed within the limitations of a prison may be optimal for those conditions: deep knowledge of a collection, working within a curated set of sources, patiently planning ahead because sources are not immediately available. But they aren't the skills the same student will need upon release, when they find themselves in an environment saturated with overwhelming, unfiltered and algorithmically sorted information. Teach only for the inside environment and you set people up for a cliff. Teach only for the outside one and find students struggle to engage with something they only see as useful in the distant future.

## What I'm doing about it right now

Very little of this is theoretical. The early work:

- **Assessment:** Documenting the technological infrastructure that supports incarcerated students, from intranet systems and lab hardware to learning platforms, library technologies, and approved research tools, then mapping where the gaps limit access to academic material. This is much of what I'm doing at the moment.
- Co-designing surveys, focus groups, and infrastructural assessments with students and faculty. I'm a month in. The people who have been teaching and studying in this context already know what breaks; my job is to collect that systematically rather than arrive with a solution.
- **Writing it down**, in the form of a programmatic knowledge base of approved technologies, technical skills, and offline research resources, so a faculty member designing a course or a student starting a project doesn't have to rediscover the constraints from scratch every semester.
- **Coordination** with the NYU Division of Libraries, the New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision, and peer college-in-prison providers. Several institutions are working on overlapping pieces of this in parallel.
- Developing resources for formerly incarcerated students continuing their education after release, which is where the transfer gap stops being abstract.

## The part I have to unlearn

My background is machine learning, data engineering, and physics. In that world you assume a package index, a docs site, a GPU you can reach, a Stack Overflow answer waiting for whatever error message you just hit. Nearly every tool I'm fluent in was built on the premise that the network is there.

The instinct that does transfer, I think, is the experimental one: characterize the channel before you try to send anything through it. In my graduate work that meant measuring a noisy physical medium carefully enough to know what it could carry. Here the medium is institutional rather than physical, but the discipline is the same, and the alternative is designing for an environment that doesn't exist.

More once the assessment work turns up things I didn't see coming.
